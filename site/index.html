Space Goats: Beyond the Table Ladders

The Funny Goat Bits →

The era of the GBP 330 million band-aid is ending.

For decades, the Great SQL Monoculture has kept our enterprise data tied up like obstinate goats in a paddock, facing different directions and resolutely refusing to mingle. And for decades, the insurance industry and the NHS have paid a staggering premium to external consultants just to tell us what those isolated goats are thinking.

Five Pathways. One Problem.

Across the NHS, clinicians make life-and-death decisions without seeing the full picture. The data exists. It just does not move.

Stroke thrombolysis. 100,000 strokes a year. The stroke team has 4.5 hours. Thrombolysis hinges on one question: is this patient on anticoagulants? The answer is held by the GP. The stroke team cannot access it. 1.9 million neurons die per minute of delay.

Post-discharge suicide. The first week after psychiatric discharge is the highest-risk period. But the discharge event does not push a notification to the community team. The 72-hour clock ticks and nobody has started it.

Section 136 crisis care. A person is detained under the Mental Health Act. The receiving clinician cannot see their GP medications, their liver disease, their depot injection schedule. Five systems. Zero push.

Ambulance crews flying blind. Police are withdrawing from mental health calls. The ambulance crew arrives with no crisis plan, no risk assessment, no medication list. 200 to 250 additional incidents per day.

Community nursing handover. A district nurse visits a discharged patient. The discharge summary has not arrived. The GP has not been notified. The nurse phones the hospital, gets put on hold, makes a clinical judgement.

Same structural problem as the insurance 95%. The connections were thrown away before anyone could use them.

In January 2026, a coroner directed NHS England to explain why hospital doctors cannot access GP records. The data exists in every case. It just does not move.

Prevention is better than cure.

A connected graph does not speed up the reaction. It removes the need for one. The graph that knows a patient's history can see what is coming before the crisis arrives.

See the past. See the future.

The NHS 10-Year Health Plan calls for three shifts: hospital to community, analogue to digital, treatment to prevention. A connected information layer is the infrastructure for the third shift.

The data already exists - in GP Connect, FHIR, RiO, SystmOne, EMIS. The standards already exist - FHIR UK Core, openEHR, GP Connect APIs. What is missing is someone who thinks in connected structures showing NHS teams how to see their data as a joined-up picture instead of isolated tables.

This is not a mental health problem. It is an architecture problem. And it is the same architecture problem whether the clock is measured in 72 hours for a discharged patient, 4.5 hours for a stroke, or two seconds for an insurance claim.

The Two-Second Claim

A customer files a claim. In one documented case, Lemonade's AI read it, checked the policy, ran 18 fraud algorithms, and sent the money in two seconds. The same claim at a typical UK insurer takes 25 to 30 days.

Root Insurance connects drivers to their driving through live telematics. Revenue: $1.52 billion, up 29% in a year. Allianz Australia processes simple storm claims in under 5 minutes. AXA Switzerland reports greater than 99% accuracy in automated claims triage on routine cases.

Four companies. Four different tech stacks. One thing in common: they kept the connections in their data alive.

But the 5% of companies that are actually succeeding with AI - the Lemonades, the Roots, the forward-thinking institutions - have cracked the code. They are not buying better algorithms or more expensive black boxes. They are building better environments. They are tearing down the fences, unclipping the carabiners, and letting their data connect in rich, semantic graphs.

Six Times

McKinsey found that AI-leading insurers created 6.1 times the total shareholder return of laggards over five years. Not 6.1 percent. Six times.

The AI in insurance market is projected to reach $175 to $303 billion by 2035. Deloitte projects $160 billion in savings from fraud analytics alone. That money will go somewhere. The only question is whose AI can reason about their business when it arrives.

Most organisations sit somewhere in between - running pilots, connecting some data, still fighting legacy SQL in other corners. The gradient is real. But the direction matters more than the position. If your competitor is pulling toward the 5% and you are standing still, they are not slightly ahead. They are in a different race.

Most importantly, they are handing the keys back to their own people.

Artificial intelligence is not a software subscription you can rent from an external vendor; it is a structural capability you must cultivate from within. When you teach your underwriters, your clinicians, and your claims handlers the true shape of the data, the magic returns in-house.

What the 95% Look Like

A customer has motor, pet, and home policies with the same insurer. Her house is 200 metres from a flood plain. Her motor renewal is in six weeks. Five systems hold these facts. None of them know the others exist.

The renewal goes out at the standard rate. No bundle. No flood risk adjustment. The customer gets a better offer from a competitor and leaves. The insurer loses the household.

Multiply by a million customers and you have the 95%. The connections were thrown away before the AI ever saw the data. Not deliberately. They simply did not fit the shape. A spreadsheet has no column for "is married to" or "lives near a flood plain."

You no longer need to outsource your corporate memory. The AI becomes explainable, and treating people like people becomes a natural byproduct of your architecture. Consumer Duty - being fair. Customer Care - making people feel listened to. Duty of Care - the whole-person responsibility the NHS carries every day. These are not compliance checkboxes; they are what connected data does automatically when the relationships are intact. And the multi-million-pound consulting dependency simply evaporates.

The 95% will keep writing blank cheques to external vendors and wondering why their pilots continuously fail to reach production.

The 5% will connect their data, empower their staff, and own their intelligence.

The other 95% are still extracting it into rows, one cell at a time, and wondering why the model cannot tell them anything they did not already know.

Sovereign Capability

The goats are still tethered to their separate posts. A semantic overlay does not move them. It draws a map in the dirt between them. Nothing breaks. Nothing migrates. But now the AI can see the relationships.

On 3 June 2026, Parliament recommended the government exercise the break clause on the GBP 330 million NHS Palantir contract. The committee branded it "an unacceptable point of weakness." The government announced a contract review on 15 June.

The NHS does not need another platform it rents from a foreign defence contractor. It needs sovereign capability - the ability to connect its own data, with its own people, using open standards it already has. FHIR. GP Connect. OpenEHR. OpenSAFELY-style federation where code goes to data, not data to code.

We offer training, not a platform. There is nothing to buy. No vendor dependency. No lock-in. The NHS owns everything built.

Where to Start

A minimal semantic layer is not a multi-year transformation programme. It is a team of two or three people, one business domain, and twelve weeks.

Pick one domain - claims, renewals, referrals - where the pain of disconnection is already costing you money. Map the entities and their relationships. Not all of them. Just the ones your people already navigate in their heads every day.

Build the overlay - a graph that sits alongside your existing systems. Nothing migrates. Nothing breaks. The graph reads from the sources you already have and draws the connections between them.

Let your people use it - the underwriter who currently checks four screens can now ask one question and get a connected answer. The claims handler sees the customer's whole world, not one policy at a time.

A pilot costs GBP 200,000 to 400,000. Governance is lighter than you think - the graph is explainable by design. Every answer traces back through visible relationships to source records. No black box.
